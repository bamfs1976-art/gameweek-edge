How Fantasy EFL works

Fantasy EFL is the English Football League's official fantasy game, played across the Championship, League One and League Two. It asks you for three things every round: seven players, two clubs and one captain. This page is a short guide to those decisions and how the tools on this site help with them. It is not the official rulebook — for the current scoring table, deadlines and any rule changes, always check the official game.

The seven-player selection

You pick a small team, and "small" is the whole character of the game. Seven starters means every single pick is roughly a seventh of your score, so there is nowhere to hide a passenger. In a fifteen-man Premier League squad a bad fourth defender costs you a couple of points a week. Here, it costs you a seventh of the round.

Two consequences follow, and both run against the instincts a Premier League fantasy game teaches you:

  • Minutes matter more than upside. A player who might get you eight points but might not start is a much worse pick than one who will certainly play and will probably get four. Check starts before you check goals — the player finder shows starts and minutes on every row for exactly this reason.
  • Spreading risk is harder. With seven players you cannot cover every outcome, so a coherent squad — players from clubs with genuinely good fixtures — usually beats a scattering of individually clever picks.

The two club picks

Alongside your players you choose two clubs, and this is where most managers leave points behind. Club picks are quietly a fixture bet: you are backing a team's whole performance rather than an individual's, which means the things that decide them are more predictable than the things that decide a player's return. Form, defensive record and the difficulty of the next match do most of the work.

They are also the easiest part of the game to set once in August and never look at again. Don't. A club with three straight home games against the bottom of its division is a different proposition from the same club a month later. The club picker ranks all 72 on form, goals scored and conceded, clean sheets, home and away records and the fixtures ahead — and shows the reasoning, so you can override it when you know something it does not.

The captain

Your captain's return counts for more than anyone else's, which makes it the single highest-variance decision you make each round. The instinct is to captain the best player you own. The better habit is to captain the best situation you own: a strong player, at home, against a side conceding regularly, who is certain to start.

That last clause does more work than any other. A captain who is rested after a midweek game does not score you half of nothing — he scores you nothing, twice. Check the fixture ticker for short turnarounds before you commit the armband.

Fixture difficulty

Every fixture on this site carries a rating from 1 (most favourable) to 5 (toughest). It is built from the opponent's points per game, their defensive record and their attacking record, adjusted for home advantage — and it is calculated within a division. A rating of 2 in League Two means "comfortably easier than average for League Two", not "easier than a Championship fixture".

That division-local approach is deliberate, and it is what makes cross-division comparison possible at all. If ratings were calculated across all 72 clubs at once, every League Two fixture would look easy, every Championship fixture would look hard, and the ticker would just be telling you which division you were reading.

Fixture ratings are a modelled guide, not a guarantee. They are built from results so far and cannot see a manager change, an injury crisis, a cup replay or a club that has quietly been much better than its table position for a month.

Short-term form, starts and minutes

Form on this site means the last five rounds, and nothing longer. Over a 46-game season a player's total points tell you who has been good since August; the last five tell you who is good now, and only one of those is a reason to pick someone this weekend.

Form is read alongside minutes rather than instead of them. Twelve points in two appearances is a different signal from twelve in five, and a player averaging 60 minutes is a different proposition from one who plays 90 every week. The finder shows points per appearance, starts, minutes and availability side by side so the three can be read together.

Availability

The cheapest points in any fantasy game are the ones you don't lose to a player who was never going to play. Every player on this site carries a status — available, doubtful, injured, suspended or unavailable — and the modelled rating treats it as a multiplier rather than a minor deduction. An injured player with perfect form is not a slightly worse pick; he is not a pick.

Comparing across three divisions

This is the skill Fantasy EFL asks for that no single-division game does. A forward scoring freely in League Two and a Championship midfielder with three assists in four are genuinely hard to compare, and the honest answer is that raw totals cannot do it.

So every input in the models here is normalised within a player's own division before anything is ranked: form against players in the same division and position, fixture difficulty against clubs in the same division, club form against the same twenty-four teams. What is compared across divisions is a player's standing, not their raw numbers.

Differentials

A differential normally means a player few other managers own. Fantasy EFL does not publish ownership publicly, so this site cannot tell you that, and does not pretend to — there is no ownership column anywhere, and there will not be one until there is a feed behind it.

What the finder offers instead is a form differential: an editorial, modelled score combining strong recent output with how much attention a player's club tends to get — its division, and how near the top of it the club sits. It is a prompt to go and look at someone, not evidence that they are lightly owned. It is labelled as modelled wherever it appears.

How the recommendations are built

Nothing on this site is a black box. Two weighted models do all the work, and both are in one file with the weights at the top of it.

Player rating

  • Recent form (30%) — fantasy points per appearance in the last five rounds
  • Starts and minutes (22%) — the floor under everything else
  • Output (20%) — goals, assists and clean sheets, weighted by position
  • Next fixture (20%) — the 1-5 rating above
  • Home advantage (8%)
  • Availability — applied as a multiplier over the total, not as another slice

Club rating

  • Recent form (28%) — league points in the last five
  • Upcoming fixtures (28%) — mean difficulty of the next three
  • Goals conceded (20%) — in the last five
  • Goals scored (17%) — in the last five
  • Home matches (7%) — how many of the next three are at home

Both produce a score out of 100 and a plain-English sentence assembled from whichever factors contributed most — "strong recent form, a favourable home fixture and three consecutive starts" is a summary of the arithmetic, not a caption written over it. Where a score is shown, the reasoning is shown with it.

These are ratings, not predictions. They rank the options in front of you against stated criteria using results that have already happened. They cannot see team news, weather, a dressing-room row or a manager who has decided to rest half a side before a cup tie — which is exactly the sort of thing you know and they do not. Use them as a shortlist and then apply what you know.
